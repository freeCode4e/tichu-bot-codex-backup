# Tichu strategy feedback and experiment queue

Keep this note with the project so new work can build on the player's feedback without asking them to repeat it. Record the player's explicit requirements as rules to implement and test; treat unconfirmed strategy ideas as hypotheses.

## Current experiment: passed-card knowledge and partner signaling

- Give each policy seat its exact outgoing pass and the exact physical card it received from its partner. Do not expose the partner's passes to other seats or an opponent's private passes.
- Track public played cards by seat so a known passed card stops being treated as held once it appears in play.
- When the same legal combination can be made with another card of the same rank, spend the alternate card and preserve the exact card received from the partner. This includes a pair from three Aces. Passing remains an option; if no equivalent combination exists, including a unique bomb, the received card can be used.
- Use shared pass knowledge to locate high cards. If all unplayed Aces are in the acting player's hand or known to be with their partner, test leading Kings; opponents then need the Dragon or a bomb to stop them.
- Evaluate both the behavior itself (preservation of the exact passed card and Kings played after Aces are accounted for) and match outcomes. Use matched deal seeds and more than one opponent policy.

## Hand strength, leads, and Tichu calls

- Count the leads needed to dispose of the hand and the leads likely to be won. Account for the strength and number of high cards and strong combinations, low cards that need an exit, opponent Aces, Dragon, Phoenix, Bird, and plausible bombs.
- A hand such as AA 34 is weaker than AA 98 because its remaining low singles are harder to dispose of. More high cards that must be shed also lower the chance that a Tichu call succeeds.
- Delay a Tichu call in some hands to learn from the first plays. Player hand sizes and cards already seen can make the call easier to judge.
- A hand with Dragon, Bird, and a strong combination may force multiple leads. Test ordered play sequences, including preserving the combination for the end and using high singles to draw out opponent Aces.
- Consider opponent leads as well as team leads: an opponent may still hold one or two Aces and interrupt an apparently forced sequence.
- When a partner called Tichu, read the exact play that forced them to spend a high card. For example, an Ace played over a Jack suggests they lack a higher single; an Ace played over a Two may indicate they lack a useful low single. Use that signal to decide whether to protect their remaining winners, make opponents spend Aces, or take the lead yourself.
- Use partner-hit cost as a gradient: hitting a partner above Ten is usually poor; taking a lower partner card becomes more acceptable as its rank falls. If the partner called Tichu, passing may protect their plan. An Ace can remove an opponent's high single and tell the caller an Ace is gone, while spending that Ace can also reduce gammon chances or expose low cards to the Dragon holder.

## Passing and information

- Passing the best card to partner can create useful signals, but always doing so can weaken the sender's hand. Compare it with a strategy that preserves the sender's hand, and test a mix based on hand strength and expected leads.
- Consider passing an Ace, King, Queen, Dragon, Bird, Dog, or a card that completes a possible bomb based on the caller's hand, remaining leads, and whether a Tichu or Grand Tichu was already called.
- Do not judge passes by the sent card alone: a pass can strengthen the partner while breaking the sender's hand. For example, with KKKQ, test whether passing a Queen is better than passing a King because the Queen may be more useful for a bomb; vary the choice with how strong the sender's hand already is.
- A partner who received an Ace may decline to hit a high single, letting the other partner keep an Ace. Once Aces are out or located, Kings and Queens may become team leads.
- Track the exact physical cards passed to opponents and use public plays to update which of them remain. Use only the cards received by the acting player to infer opponent pass tendencies; never assume access to what the partner passed to opponents.
- Use known passed cards in endgames. If an opponent still holds a specific passed Two, then plays the Dragon with that Two as their last card, a bomb may stop them from going out; then consider a low lead that forces the opponent's partner to spend winners. Time that bomb based on our hand and the remaining leads.
- Test wishing for low cards such as Fives or Sixes to disrupt a caller, and compare with Ace wishes using the cards passed, score, hand shape, and Dragon/Bird/Dog locations.
- With three Aces and a King, an Ace wish may draw an opponent's Ace before leading the King. Compare that with lower wishes and with whether an Ace was passed to partner.
- If the partner called Tichu, passing may be more valuable than taking a lead, depending on the rank just played and the partner's likely remaining hand.

## Combination play, Birds, and one-two finishes

- Playing the Mahjong as part of a straight is often useful. Test when to split pairs such as Kings, Queens, or Jacks into singles to create leads.
- A low full house can draw high cards but risks handing the lead to opponents if beaten. Preserve a full house for an exit sequence when the other leads allow it; test sequences such as King, Phoenix over an opponent's Ace, Two, Dragon, then the full house, with bomb risk accounted for.
- Consider saving the Bird for a late single or Tichu exit. If the caller goes out with the Bird, the partner can take the lead if opponents pass; if opponents hit, the team can decide whether another response helps. Avoid spending the Bird inside a combination when that unnecessarily opens a lead to an opponent.
- Getting out with the Bird can avoid losing its 25 points, but it may give opponents a chance to hit and take the lead. Compare going out with it against saving it for a later single, especially when the partner can immediately win the trick.
- Coordinate a one-two finish. For example, if the partner can go out with a shorter straight and retain a single, test whether the single exit gives the Dragon holder a safer chance to finish. A partner with Dragon and Two may then take the lead and go out.
- Track the exact remaining cards, hand sizes, and bombs. When only two players remain, public cards can reveal their full hands; use that information for both players' decisions.
- A Dog may be worth keeping or passing to the Tichu caller depending on hand strength. If the partner passed the Mahjong, a weak hand may use the Dog to transfer the lead; a strong hand may want to preserve it.
- If a weak hand receives the Mahjong from partner, playing the Dog can return the lead to them; if the hand is strong, passing the Mahjong to partner may let them use the Dog instead. Test holding the Dog versus passing it to the Grand/Tichu caller to support or disrupt their plan.

Passing remains available, and a unique combination using the card is still legal. The King's future-lead behavior remains a separate hypothesis to measure.
- Before generalizing preservation beyond single Aces, a 100-match evaluation with the preservation score bonus set to -8 kept the partner-passed Ace on all 10 eligible single-Ace plays. ## Follow-up: known winners are options, not instructions (2026-11-20)

- Correct the earlier King test: knowing that opponents cannot beat a King with an Ace means the King is a likely future lead. It does not mean the bot should play that King immediately. The previous positive King-action prior tested an immediate King play and missed this strategy.
- Keep a count and location model for known Aces, Dragon, and Bird using only each seat's own pass, the exact card received from partner, and public plays. If a team passed Dragon to partner and received Bird, or vice versa, that can establish who holds a future lead. If the passes were not informative, keep the location uncertain.
- Use known winners when deciding whether to call Tichu: compare leads the team can likely take with the low singles and combinations (losers) that still need to be shed. Two Aces are not automatically two leads while Dragon or Bird can still take them; if those special cards are known to be with partner or are already played, the lead count can change.
- When an opponent leads a single, prefer the cheapest useful response, often one rank higher, if that card is not part of a planned combination. Consider passing or spending a low loser instead of breaking a straight or another strong combination. Sometimes breaking a weak hand early is correct, especially when partner is known to hold Dragon and can win after the opponents spend their low cards.
- Think of singles as winners, high cards, middle cards, and low losers, while marking cards committed to combinations. A Nine inside a planned straight is not available as a free Nine single. A low triple that should be played as a pair of twos with a separate triple may leave a three as a loser worth shedding when the opportunity is safe.
- Read what each play says about the hand. If a partner spends an Ace after a low single, infer which singles they may lack; if they spend it over a Jack, infer they lack higher singles. Use that to decide whether to hit an opponent, preserve partner winners, or help them discard low cards. If both partners keep passing to protect combinations, the weaker hand may need to break first.
- Continue the exact-card endgame reasoning: if a player holds Dragon plus two known passed cards, work out the plausible remaining hands and bomb/out risks before acting. A Dragon may be bombed when the remaining cards prove the player cannot get out; a low lead after the bomb can then force their partner to spend winners. Consider other unseen threes, Aces, Bird, and bomb possibilities before concluding.
- Once enough Aces and Kings have been played, update whether a Queen full house is now unbeatable by ordinary combinations. Use passed physical cards when choosing between rank-equivalent cards in a full house or pair so opponents learn less. If two combinations are similarly strong and a bomb is possible, prefer the sequence that still has a route out if one is bombed. A high triplet may sometimes be better split into singles to create leads.
- Measure whether the bot preserves a known King as a future lead while disposing of a low loser, then whether it spends that King when the lead is valuable. Track Tichu success and one-two finishes alongside match scores. The prior experiment's immediate-King frequency alone does not test this.

## Ordering loose singles and blocking passed ranks (2026-11-20)

- The default order for loose singles is low to high: when a 9 can safely beat an 8, spend the loose 9 and keep the King as a stronger future winner. A King in the hand makes the hand stronger, but that static strength alone does not encode the best order for shedding cards.
- “Low to high” applies to loose losers the hand needs to shed; it is not an absolute rule to spend every lower card first. Spend an Ace, King, Queen, Dragon, Bird, or strong combination early when it stops an opponent's Tichu or 1-2, guarantees an immediate exit, or unlocks a likely go-out sequence.
- Count winners against an actual exit plan, not just one winner per leftover card. For example, Dragon plus a low single and a 2-6 straight can be one lead away from going out if the straight is likely to hold and the low single can follow. If the straight is likely to be beaten, the same hand needs another lead or a different plan.
- Respect the hand plan: a 9 that belongs to a straight is committed, not a free single. Do not break a planned combination simply to use the 9; compare another loose card, passing, or asking the weaker teammate to break first.
- Add an exception using pass information. If the bot can lead a loose 2 or a loose 3, and knows it passed an unplayed 3 to an opponent, leading the 3 may block that opponent from answering with the equal 3 and shedding it. Test this against leading the 2, accounting for higher responses, hand structure, and whether the lead is useful. Blocking is less valuable for a higher passed card that is already hard for the opponent to shed.
- When responding to a single, prefer the lowest uncommitted card that can take it when winning the trick is useful. A high King should stay in the winner budget unless the hand plan or current trick makes spending it valuable.
- When our partner cannot take the current play, it may be right to break even a strong combination to take the lead, shed a loser, and force opponents to spend cards. When partner has a known winner, also test passing so partner can take the lead later. If one opponent is out or has only a few cards, weak combinations are less likely to be hit by both opponents and may become useful exit plays.
- Treat single-card order, known opponent pass ranks, and committed combinations as a focused next test. Measure cards shed, winners spent, tricks taken, Tichu success, and one-two finishes; do not infer success from overall hand-strength scores alone.

## Follow-up: pass signals, exit plans, and hidden bombs (2026-11-20)

- Preserve the partner's exact received card whenever a same-rank replacement makes the same legal combination. With three Aces and a pair play, use the other two Aces; with four Aces, preserve the received Ace while using other equivalent Aces when the selected combination allows it. Do not confuse this identity choice with whether to play or pass.
- Treat a partner's best-card passing strategy as a source of information, not certainty. If one player receives a King and the other receives the Dragon, reason about which high cards may remain on each side, then update that inference from public plays. A partner may use a passed-card signal to decide whether to spend a King, protect a low card, or wait for a safer exit.
- Plan one-two finishes from actual remaining cards and likely responses. For A-2 or K-2 endings, compare going out on the high card now (so a hit may let partner finish) with waiting when an opponent may hold the winner. Account for whether the partner can take the lead and whether Dragon, Bird, and Aces are already located or played.
- Test Mahjong wishes for Aces as a way to draw out opponents' Aces, followed by a full house, triple, or pair that forces more high singles out. With four Aces, consider whether a wish could expose the whole bomb and whether taking leads with Kings first protects a better sequence. Keep the hand shape, bomb value, likely wish fulfillment, and partner's ability to go out in the comparison.
- Use suit and rank history to estimate straight-flush bomb possibilities. When passing an Ace to partner, compare suits that could join their likely sequences; when choosing an equivalent card to play, consider whether spending a card from a common suit conceals another suit sequence. Balance this information against breaking the sender's own straight or bomb potential.
- The policy round context now tracks the exact card received from the partner and from each opponent, plus the acting player's three outgoing cards. It still does not know the partner's other two outgoing passes or any opponent-to-opponent pass.
- When a legal combination can substitute an equivalent same-rank card, preserve the exact partner-passed card and use an opponent-passed copy. Passing stays available, and this identity rule does not block a unique combination or bomb.
- Tichu declarations still use a learned linear score over hand counts and public context. They do not search ordered exit plans or estimate the chance each planned lead survives opponents' responses. Knowing pass provenance does not make the declaration policy use it automatically; those estimates need explicit features or a planner and then a useful policy/heuristic over them.

## Offline Tichu call analysis (2026-11-21)

- The acting seat now receives its own exact outgoing pass and the exact card received from its partner, right opponent, and left opponent. Public plays are recorded by seat. Equivalent same-rank moves preserve the partner's physical card and use opponent-passed physical copies when possible.
- `--analyze-tichu-hand` compares minimum-play partitions with candidates up to one additional play. It samples 128 unseen deals, fixes unplayed cards passed out by the actor to their recipients, and tests each candidate lead for legal opponent responses, including bombs. It reports likely winners, likely losers, low singles, and known outgoing pass locations.
- This is a diagnostic only. Each candidate lead is tested independently; the analysis does not model the order in which cards are played, opponents spending cards between leads, the partner's response, or the inference that comes from an opponent's pass choice. It does not change Tichu calls.
- Next, build a response sequence model around the player's guidance: save the Dragon when a partner or opponent can create a better lead, spend intermediate winners when the opponent may go out, and use the caller's lead budget to shed low cards. Compare plans that use the same cards in different orders, then validate those scenarios before connecting estimates to call decisions.
- Do not spend the Dragon automatically when an opponent leads a King. If the partner can hit and shed a low card, preserving the Dragon may leave a safer final lead. If the opponent is the right-hand opponent who received a useful pass, or appears close to going out, hitting may be necessary. When the opponent leads a Queen, a King can be the cheaper response; save stronger winners unless the situation calls for them.
- Track when a Tichu caller is forced to use a King or other intermediate winner. The remaining hand may become awkward if that winner was needed to shed a 10 or protect a later low lead. Evaluate the response order against who has the next turn, what the partner can win with, and whether the opponent is likely to throw a low card or a go-out combination.

## Phoenix responses and owner-aware play (2026-11-21)

- In this project, “Bird” means the Phoenix. Prefer the name Phoenix in code, tests, and analysis so it cannot be confused with another card.
- Separate the Phoenix's lead value from its response value. As a single lead it is weak (strength 1.5); responding to an ordinary single resolves it to half a rank above that card, so it can beat Mahjong and every rank from Two through Ace. It cannot beat Dragon, and Dog transfers the lead without a response. A Phoenix in hand is therefore a conditional response resource, not a guaranteed lead by itself.
- Update that response estimate as cards are played or located. If Dragon is already out or known to be on the acting team's side, the Phoenix is safer against ordinary single leads. If an opponent may still hold Dragon, their Dragon can retake the trick. Account for whose turn follows and who owns the current top before deciding whether to spend a King, Phoenix, Ace, or Dragon.
- The engine state records the owner of every public play. The learned move and Tichu-call feature vectors now expose whether the current top belongs to the partner, right opponent, or left opponent, along with the top combination's strength and shape and each opponent's hand size. This gives training the needed distinction; it does not itself plan multiple plays or infer every hidden hand.
- For Tichu calls, compare complete exit plans: identify low losers, cards committed to combinations, and likely winning leads, then test whether the order sheds the losers before the remaining winners are spent. A high-card response can draw out an Ace; Phoenix may then overtake that Ace after Dragon is no longer a threat. Preserve a route to use Phoenix in a straight or other strong combination when that combination is needed to go out.
- Use the play order to protect information from partner. If holding multiple Aces, normally play another Ace before the one partner passed, preserving the signal that the passed Ace is still held. Apply the same same-rank preservation rule to pairs and other legal combinations. When a player uses the passed Ace, partner can infer that no additional Ace was available to play first.
- Compare lower and higher full houses using the exact top owner, caller's remaining cards, known bomb risk, the team's route to a one-two finish, and score needs. A lower full house can save a higher one for a later decisive trick; spending the higher one immediately can be correct when it stops a near-out Tichu caller or opens a safe exit. Track points taken as well as who goes out first and whether the team finishes one-two.
- Turn these cases into repeatable replay scenarios. Compare alternative move orders on the same known state and sampled hidden hands, then report response frequency, low cards shed, winners spent, Tichu success, one-two finishes, points captured, and match score. The current call analyzer still evaluates candidate leads independently and does not yet simulate these multi-turn sequences or partner responses; keep it diagnostic until sequence results are tested.

## Paired sequence replay diagnostic (2026-11-21)

- The analyzer now replays alternative orders for up to the two highest-ranked hand partitions on the same 12 sampled deals. It compares the partition's enumerated order, low lead strength first, a King/Phoenix/low-single/Dragon ordering, and high lead strength first, removing duplicate orders.
- Each order is replayed with both a random partner and a caller-support partner. The support heuristic usually passes over the caller, can shed a low single against a low opposing single, can answer Q-or-higher with an Ace when the lead comes from the opponent immediately before the partner, may answer a pair when the caller has at most two cards, and may lead Dog to transfer the lead to a near-out caller. The two opponents use the random baseline.
- During each replay, the caller follows the selected order when leading and can spend one of its remaining planned combinations to answer an opponent. It passes over the partner's play unless it can go out. The caller's Tichu is scored; the report includes caller-first and caller-second rates, team one-two rate, and average team point margin.
- These figures compare orders and partner-response assumptions under simple policies; they are not expert-play odds. The replay does not yet model other players' calls, strategic opponent urgency, partner inference from all pass choices, or passing behavior. The small 12-deal sample is not enough for strong match-level conclusions.
- Ordered replay currently supports only the initial post-pass state with fourteen cards in every hand. Mid-play snapshots still receive independent lead estimates only. Keep the result diagnostic; do not connect it to Tichu calls until the response model and sampled results are improved.
- First paired CLI run on the README example hand: the random-partner orders had caller-first rates of 92%, team one-two rates of 17-25%, and average team point margins of +95 to +111. The caller-support orders had caller-first rates of 100%, team one-two rates of 0%, and margins of +53 to +71. Each figure uses only 12 sampled deals and shares deals across ordering comparisons. This does not prove the heuristic is worse, but it flags a useful tradeoff to inspect: it may help the caller finish while leaving the partner behind or collecting fewer points. Add a public-action replay trace before drawing conclusions or asking for strategy feedback.
